Features

What it does.

Reading, questions, flashcards and practice exams sit in one plan. Press Start and the next thing is ready.

Plan

A plan for every day until the exam.

Skopos builds a day-by-day schedule from your exam date and the hours you have, with catch-up and rest days. Miss a day and it offers three ways back: catch up slowly, study longer each day, or move the exam date.

The plan is not stored. It is recalculated from your real progress every time, so it does not go stale.

Questions

Questions in the AAMC exam format.

Passage-based questions in the AAMC format, with figures, equations and a clock. The toolbar has highlight, strikethrough, flag for review and the periodic table, as in the official test layout.

Explanations

Step-by-step explanations for wrong answers.

Every wrong answer is explained step by step: why the option you chose does not hold, and why the correct one works. In two independent blind AI rating runs, these explanations scored 4.87 out of 5.

Flashcards

Each card returns before you would forget it.

Rate how well you knew a card and Skopos schedules the next review with FSRS, an open spaced-repetition algorithm. Each button shows when the card will come back.

Anki decks import and export. A deck of over 6,000 notes imports in under 30 seconds.

Sources

Every card traces back to the textbook.

Science content comes from OpenStax textbooks. Each card shows the section it came from, and every textbook figure carries its source and licence. The built-in reader opens the full section.

Progress

How much of each topic you are likely to remember.

Progress shows how much of each topic you are likely to remember today, and which topics need a review. One button turns that list into a short session.

Practice exams

Timed full-length and single-section exams.

Sit a full-length exam or a single section under exam conditions, with timed breaks, and a closed section does not reopen. Plan one for a set day and it joins your schedule.

Why it is free

Why Skopos is free.

MCAT preparation can cost thousands of dollars before a student applies to a single school. About half of US medical students come from the top fifth of household incomes. Doctors also tend to practise in communities like the ones they grew up in. When cost decides who can prepare, it also decides which communities will have doctors who understand them.

Skopos is free so that access does not depend on income. It does not require an account, and it does not show ads or sell your data.

It is built on open education. The science comes from OpenStax, the free, peer-reviewed textbooks from Rice University, and the CARS passages come from open-access writing. Good preparation should be open to anyone willing to do the work, and Skopos will stay free.

Hydrostatic Pressure and Fluid Column Dynamics

Hydrostatic pressure reflects the force per unit area exerted by the weight of a static fluid column. In medical applications such as open-tube manometers and intravenous (IV) infusion lines, the hydrostatic gauge pressure generated by a fluid column of height h and density ρ under gravitational acceleration g is expressed by Equation 1. Because the free surface of the fluid in these open systems is exposed to the atmosphere, atmospheric pressure acts on that surface, so the absolute pressure at depth h equals the gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure, as defined by Equation 2.

In Experiment 1, researchers measured the hydrostatic gauge pressure (Pg) at various depths h below the open fluid surface within vertical cylindrical columns open to the atmosphere (Patm = 1.01 × 105 Pa, g = 9.8 m/s2). Two test fluids were evaluated: normal saline (ρ = 1.00 × 103 kg/m3) and an iodinated radiographic contrast solution (ρ = 1.36 × 103 kg/m3). As Table 1 shows, gauge pressure was recorded at depths of 0.25 m, 0.50 m, and 1.00 m.

In Experiment 2, researchers connected the saline reservoir to a compliant vessel simulator maintained at an internal venous gauge pressure of 2400 Pa (equivalent to approximately 18 mm Hg). The vertical height of the reservoir's fluid surface above the vessel entry point was adjusted incrementally to determine the minimum height required to initiate flow from the reservoir into the vessel. Fluid entry into the vessel commenced only when this vertical height equaled or exceeded 0.245 m.

Adapted from College Physics, OpenStax, CC BY 4.0.

Question 1 of 4. A researcher suspects that the cylindrical shape of the columns used in Experiment 1 affected the hydrostatic gauge pressure results. To evaluate this hypothesis while isolating container geometry as the independent variable, the researcher should:

Why A is wrong

Varying fluid density in uniform cylindrical columns tests the effect of density rather than vessel geometry, replicating the comparison between saline and contrast agent already conducted in Experiment 1.

Why C is wrong

Sealing the columns alters the surface boundary condition and ambient pressure transmission, failing to evaluate whether cross-sectional geometry affects hydrostatic gauge pressure.

Why D is wrong

Allowing fluid depths to vary when changing column diameter introduces depth as an uncontrolled confounding variable, preventing direct isolation of container geometry.

Why B is right

Hydrostatic gauge pressure depends on fluid density, gravitational acceleration, and depth below the surface according to Equation 1 (Pg = ρgh), independent of vessel shape or cross-sectional area. To test whether column geometry influences the pressure measurements, the experimental design must vary the container geometry while controlling all other variables by holding depth, density, and ambient exposure constant.

Question 2 of 4. If the researchers in Experiment 2 replaced the normal saline solution with the iodinated contrast agent evaluated in Experiment 1, what minimum reservoir height above the vessel entry point would be required to initiate flow into the vessel simulator?

Why A is wrong

This value represents the reduction in required height relative to saline (Δh = 0.245 m - 0.18 m = 0.065 m ≈ 0.07 m), confusing the difference between the two configurations with the absolute height required for the contrast agent.

Why B is wrong

Equation 1 indicates that hydrostatic pressure depends linearly on fluid density (Pg = ρgh). Scaling the column height inversely with the square of relative density rather than its first power produces 0.13 m.

Why D is wrong

This value arises from treating column height as directly proportional to fluid density (multiplying 0.245 m by 1.36) rather than recognizing the inverse relationship h = Pg / (ρg).

Why C is right

To initiate fluid flow into the vessel simulator, the hydrostatic gauge pressure generated by the fluid column must reach the opposing venous gauge pressure of 2400 Pa. Rearranging Equation 1 (Pg = ρgh) for column height gives h = Pg / (ρg). Substituting the density of the iodinated contrast agent from Table 1 (1.36 × 103 kg/m3) and gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s2) yields h = 2400 Pa / ((1.36 × 103 kg/m3)(9.8 m/s2)) ≈ 0.18 m.

Question 3 of 4. Based on Equation 2 and Table 1, what was the absolute pressure at a depth of 1.00 m in the saline column during Experiment 1 (note: 1 kPa = 103 Pa)?

Why A is wrong

This choice mistakes the hydrostatic gauge pressure recorded in Table 1 (9.80 kPa = 9.80 × 103 Pa) for absolute pressure, omitting the atmospheric pressure term required by Equation 2.

Why B is wrong

This value results from subtracting the fluid column's gauge pressure from atmospheric pressure (1.01 × 105 Pa − 9.80 × 103 Pa), whereas absolute pressure requires adding the two pressures.

Why C is wrong

This choice considers only atmospheric pressure acting at the open surface (1.01 × 105 Pa), neglecting the additional hydrostatic pressure exerted by the 1.00 m column of saline.

Why D is right

Equation 2 defines absolute pressure as the sum of gauge pressure and atmospheric pressure (Pabs = Pg + Patm). In Experiment 1, atmospheric pressure is given as 1.01 × 105 Pa, and Table 1 reports the hydrostatic gauge pressure at a depth of 1.00 m in the saline column as 9.80 kPa (9.80 × 103 Pa). Adding the atmospheric pressure to this hydrostatic contribution gives 1.01 × 105 Pa + 0.098 × 105 Pa = 1.11 × 105 Pa.

Question 4 of 4. According to Equation 1 and Table 1, how does the hydrostatic gauge pressure change when depth within the contrast agent column increases from 0.50 m to 1.00 m?

Why B is wrong

This reflects the misconception that hydrostatic pressure follows a quadratic dependence on depth. Equation 1 instead indicates a first-order linear relationship with column depth.

Why C is wrong

This value arises from mistakenly computing the fractional increase in absolute pressure by including atmospheric pressure (from approximately 107.7 kPa to 114.3 kPa), whereas the question asks specifically for gauge pressure.

Why D is wrong

This relies on the belief that a constant fluid density prevents pressure from varying. Hydrostatic gauge pressure, however, scales directly with depth regardless of uniform density.

Why A is right

Equation 1 defines hydrostatic gauge pressure as Pg = ρgh. Because density ρ and gravitational acceleration g are constant, gauge pressure is directly proportional to depth h. Doubling the depth from 0.50 m to 1.00 m doubles the gauge pressure, as reflected in Table 1 where Pg increases from 6.66 kPa to 13.33 kPa.

Through Time and Across Traits: Evaluating Explanations of Human Evolution

Human evolution lacks a widely accepted unifying explanation. It need not have one. Biological history need not resolve into a single organizing principle in the way geology is resolved into plate tectonics, and a field can be in good order without a theory of that form. What the field lacks is an agreed way of stating what kind of question a given hypothesis answers. Explanations of human evolution are numerous and often not in direct competition, because they address different questions.

Tinbergen's scheme already supplies that discipline. A complete biological explanation must answer four logically distinct questions: how a trait is produced, how it develops in the individual, what selective problem it solves, and how it evolved from ancestral states. The scheme need not be replaced or supplemented with a fifth question. One of the four, the function question, is conventionally asked in a way that cannot detect two kinds of incompleteness in the answers it returns, and both matter for any lineage showing sustained directional change.

Asked once of a single trait, the function question yields a still photograph, this is the trait, and this is the problem it answers. Where the problem does not change across generations, a still is enough. A lineage that changed across several traits for two million years requires motion as well as focus: the question must be asked through time, and the answers compared across traits.

The first addition is temporal. Asked repeatedly of a trait that continued to change, the function question either returns a different problem at each step, in which case the trajectory is a series of thresholds requiring an account of why they aligned, or the same problem at rising magnitude, in which case the problem itself grew. A gradient is therefore a selective problem whose magnitude rises because the response to it helps generate the next problem. The second addition is cross‐trait. Tinbergen's scheme is applied trait by trait and is silent on whether the answers given for different traits are one answer or several; in a lineage showing co‐directional change across many traits, that difference is the difference between a single explanation and a coincidence of separate ones.

The additions are motivated by the human record, which appears to show sustained directional change across several traits. That claim has been contested, most directly by Gould and Eldredge, and more recently by those who emphasize the mosaic and episodic character of the record and the documented decline in endocranial volume since the terminal Pleistocene. Directionality must therefore be defined before the record is interpreted, and the same standard must govern candidate explanations. Otherwise, an explanation built to explain a pattern can always be accused of having defined the pattern to suit itself.

Fossil time series can be treated as sequences of steps between successive intervals. Two independent quantities describe such a sequence: the mean step, which determines whether the trait is displaced over the long run, and the variance of the steps, which determines how violently it moves. A series is directional over an interval when the mean of its steps differs from zero, tested by asking whether a directional model fits better than an unbiased random walk or than stasis. Volatility is irrelevant to this distinction: a series with large canceling steps is not directional, whereas a series with small consistently biased steps is.

Excerpt, word for word: Miki Ben‐Dor, Ran Barkai, “Through Time and Across Traits: Evaluating Explanations of Human Evolution” (2026). CC BY 4.0.

Question 1 of 3. In the fourth paragraph, the author describes one possible trajectory as "a series of thresholds." As used there, the term "thresholds" most nearly refers to:

Why A is wrong

The passage does not treat a threshold as a ceiling. The trait in this sentence "continued to change," and each step brings a new problem rather than a limit on change.

Why C is wrong

A single problem at rising magnitude is the sentence's other branch, which the author calls a gradient rather than a series of thresholds.

Why D is wrong

Intervals belong to the final paragraph's method for measuring fossil series, not to the fourth paragraph's account of what selection posed at each step.

Why B is right

The sentence sets out two outcomes of asking the function question repeatedly of a trait that kept changing. In the first, the question "returns a different problem at each step," and only in that case does the author call the trajectory "a series of thresholds." Each threshold is therefore one of these separate selective problems, met in sequence as the trait changes. That reading also fits the rest of the phrase: the series needs "an account of why they aligned," which makes sense only when the thresholds are distinct problems whose lining up is not automatic. The other branch, a single problem at rising magnitude, is what the author goes on to call a gradient, so the thresholds are the separate problems met step by step.

Question 2 of 3. In the fifth paragraph, the author mentions "the documented decline in endocranial volume since the terminal Pleistocene." Within the argument of that paragraph and the one that follows it, the reference to this decline chiefly serves to:

Why A is wrong

The passage does not explain the decline. It appears in a list of grounds others have offered against directionality.

Why B is wrong

The decline is offered by those who contest directionality, so it weighs against the claim of sustained directional change rather than for it.

Why D is wrong

The passage does not draw a conclusion about how much brain size matters. It cites the decline as part of a dispute over direction.

Why C is right

The fifth paragraph says the human record "appears to show" sustained directional change, then reports that this claim has been contested, listing the mosaic, episodic record and the decline in endocranial volume among the grounds. A brain that shrank in recent times is evidence that at least one major trait did not keep moving one way, so the decline counts against the claim of direction. Because the direction of the record is in dispute, the author says directionality must be defined before the record is read, and the final paragraph supplies that definition: a test of whether the mean of a trait's steps differs from zero. The decline thus helps motivate the step-based test that follows.

Question 3 of 3. Suppose researchers found that the function question, asked repeatedly of brain size and of tool complexity in one hominin lineage, returned one shared problem, rising in magnitude, for both traits. In the author's terms, this finding would directly:

Why A is wrong

The author holds that directionality must be defined before the record is interpreted and tested on the steps of each series. A finding about selective problems leaves that dispute open.

Why B is wrong

The author says the scheme "need not be replaced or supplemented with a fifth question." Asking across traits is a way of asking the function question, not a new one.

Why C is wrong

The finding concerns two traits in one lineage, and the author denies that biological history needs a single principle of that form.

Why D is right

The author says that when the function question, asked repeatedly, returns the same problem at rising magnitude, "the problem itself grew," and calls such a problem a gradient. The cross-trait addition then asks whether the answers for different traits are one answer or several, which in a lineage with co-directional change separates a single explanation from "a coincidence of separate ones." In the supposed finding, brain size and tool complexity return one problem, and that problem grows. The temporal result points to a gradient, and the cross-trait result points to one answer rather than two. Together they favor a single gradient explanation for both traits over separate explanations that merely coincide.

Evidence

The research behind the study methods.

Skopos uses learning methods with strong research support. Its content is tested blind against the materials students already use.

Spaced repetition

Reviews spread over time are remembered longer than the same reviews crammed together. Skopos schedules every card with FSRS, so each one returns at the point it is about to fade.

Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (2006), Psychological Bulletin. Cepeda and colleagues (2008), Psychological Science.

Retrieval practice

Pulling an answer from memory strengthens it more than reading it again. New topics are taught in short parts, each followed by recall prompts, then practised with cards and questions.

Bjork and Bjork (2011), Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way, in Psychology and the Real World.

Planned rest

Memory consolidates between sessions, and long unbroken stretches add errors. Rest days and breaks are scheduled by clear rules, each with its stated reason and the research behind it.

How Skopos differs

Your study data stays on your computer.

Most study apps today are cloud subscriptions tied to one AI service, and they keep your data on their servers. Skopos takes the opposite approach.

The usual approach

  • An account before you can study
  • Study history stored on the company's servers
  • One cloud AI service, chosen for you
  • Requires a connection to work

Skopos

  • Does not require an account
  • Study data stays on your computer, in an encrypted SQLCipher database
  • Four AI providers, all optional, each with your own key
  • Works offline, except the optional AI features

Local and encrypted

Study records sit close to health information, and that kind of data should not leave your computer without your say. Skopos does not use cloud storage. Data does not leave the computer unless you turn that on.

AI features are optional and use your own key. Skopos works with Gemini, OpenAI, Anthropic and Groq. A router picks the model for each task within the provider, quality setting and spending limit you chose. If not one model meets every need, it tells you plainly and says what would fix it.
